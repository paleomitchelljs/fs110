# Longrich & Currie 2009, "Albertonykus borealis, a new alvarezsaur ... from the Early Maastrichtian of Alberta"

Longrich & Currie. *Cretaceous Research* 30:239-252. doi 10.1016/j.cretres.2008.07.005. Makovicky doesn't cite it. Qin 2021 does (ref 20).

File: `papers/1-s2.0-S0195667108000943-main.pdf`, 14 pages.

## Claim

- *Albertonykus borealis*, Horseshoe Canyon Formation, from the *Albertosaurus* bonebed at Dry Island. At least two individuals.
- "Albertonykus is the sister taxon of the Asian clade Mononykinae, consistent with the hypothesis that the alvarezsaurs originated in South America, and then dispersed to Asia via North America."
- Forelimbs "specialized for digging, but they are too short to permit burrowing; they were most likely used to dig into insect nests."
- Insects: "Ants were a minor part of the ecosystem during the Cretaceous, and mound-building termites do not appear until the Eocene." Proposes wood-nesting termites; borings in Horseshoe Canyon wood (Fig 14).

## Five predictions for a myrmecophage

The paper lists them and scores alvarezsaurids on each, mostly from *Shuvuuia*:
1. Anterior diastema.
2. Reduced and simplified teeth. "perhaps 50 or more needle-like teeth packed into each dentary in Shuvuuia."
3. Long, narrow jaws.
4. Weak mandibles.
5. Reduced jaw articulations.

"In all respects, alvarezsaurs conform to the morphology predicted for a myrmecophage. Myrmecophagy is the simplest explanation, indeed the only plausible explanation, for the unusual morphology of the skull and forelimbs."

[Makovicky 2026](makovicky-2026.md) speaks to number 2 for *Alnashetri*: teeth in sockets, 21 dentary positions, "not diminutive."
