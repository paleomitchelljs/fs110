# Pennaraptora

A lecture deck on troodontids, dromaeosaurids, oviraptorosaurs and early birds, built to show one skill: pulling several lines of evidence together on one question, and seeing where each line is strong, where it is thin, and how the lines interact. Each line slide puts one paper's claim (a lens cut from the printed page), that paper's own figure, and plain facts about how much material and what was assumed. Sections end on a matrix. Plan: `plan.md`. Paper notes: `notes/` (index `notes/readme.md`). PDFs: `papers/` (gitignored).

Engine copied from `../alvarez` (deck.js, parts/util, intro, walk). New parts: `js/parts/facts.js` (fact chips), `js/parts/pair.js` (a skeletal or silhouette beside a fossil, one caption under both), `js/parts/tree.js` (a drawn cladogram of a few groups; drag one group onto another to swap them; the group next to birds lights up; branches can carry gain/loss marks or labeled pills), `js/parts/dots.js` (a redrawn dot-and-range chart with a reference line).

## Order

Subgroups (each fossil beside a skeletal); phylogeny (contenders and their second toe, four trees redrawn as one diagram, rescoring); behavior (sleep, bonebed, trackways, trackmaker, solitary hunters, pack hunting, troodontid diet); reproduction (adults on nests, adult on eggs, clutch size, care in living animals, which parent, open or buried, how warm and how long, hatching order, matrix). No break slide. Feathers (feather stages, display, wings without flight, feather shape); flight (membrane wings, physics models, feathers and arms, how many origins, wing bones, matrix); brains and the origin of birds (brain, shrinking, rates); synthesis matrix; blank matrix for the students' report. Level 2 adds friends, five stages, shoulder girdle, oversampled characters.

The phylogeny trees are redrawn, not cropped: five groups (oviraptorosaurs, troodontids, dromaeosaurids, unenlagiids, avialans), each with its skeletal. Simplifications: microraptorines and eudromaeosaurs are one tip, "Dromaeosaurids"; anchiornithines, *Archaeopteryx* and scansoriopterygids are left out. Sources: Gianechini et al. 2017 and Pei et al. 2020 (troodontids with dromaeosaurids); Godefroit et al. 2013b and Cau et al. 2017 (troodontids closest), and Agnolín & Novas 2013 (unenlagiids closest), all as drawn in Agnolín et al. 2019 Fig 2; Brusatte et al. 2014 (unresolved); Agnolín et al. 2019 Fig 8 (rescored, a polytomy at the base of Pennaraptora; they list Microraptoria and Eudromaeosauria separately in it).

## Said aloud, not on the slides

Slide ids are in brackets. The judgments ("thin," "one specimen") belong here, not on the slides.

**Phylogeny**
- [contenders] Troodontids, dromaeosaurids and unenlagiids all hold toe II off the ground. Xu 2011 found the same "highly extensible" second toe in *Archaeopteryx*, one of the features that moved it next to deinonychosaurs. A shared trait that several groups have is what makes the trees hard to settle.
- [trees] Same question, four published answers; the yellow branch is whatever sits next to birds. Have students drag tips to build the other arrangements, then ask what anatomy each would need. All four come from versions of one matrix family (TWiG) or its critics, so they are not four independent tests (Hartman: reused scorings give "a false impression of confidence"). Xu 2011 is the classic case of one new specimen moving *Archaeopteryx* out of birds; Brusatte 2014 moved it back.
- [recode] The one point about method: Agnolín et al. rescored one group (unenlagiids) in the TWiG matrix and the base of Pennaraptora fell into a polytomy. Hartman's sentence says the same thing from the other side: a single step separates several different trees. Then Agnolín et al. set their tree aside and argue their preferred one from selected characters; they named most unenlagiids, so where unenlagiids go matters to them. The egg-only tree (Zelenitsky & Therrien 2008) was dropped: it has no dromaeosaurid, so it can't choose between the contenders.

**Behavior**
- [sleep] Gao 2012: "three specimens representing two troodontid species in nearly identical positions suggests that the position of these animals is not random." The burial (volcanic, perhaps a lahar) is what makes sleep the reading. Gao: "Even a low energy lahar is likely to move a small animal out of a perimortem position," so they offer "sheltering within a burrow" as an alternative.
- [herd] Funston is careful: lekking is "consistent with, but not indicative of." The adults-only age structure rests on size and fusion, with no histology. "the specific behaviour that the Avimimus death assemblage represents cannot be assessed."
- [solitary] Roach & Brinkman set the null: no living diapsid hunts in mammal-style packs, so the burden is on the pack. Their ora analogy makes predictions: mobs at kills, fighting, cannibalism, juveniles kept away. The next two slides test two of them. Frederickson's isotopes ask whether young ate different prey (the reptile pattern); Li's trackways ask whether parallel tracks are just animals following a shore. Their own hedge: "evolution often behaves in nonparsimonious ways." Weak spot: the "combat" claw in a tail comes from a scattered quarry where, by their own account, bones can't be assigned to individuals.
- [tracks] Tracks are the only line here that records live animals at the same moment. Li: "a scenario in which six similarly sized individuals independently generated parallel, regularly spaced, nonoverlapping trackways is highly unlikely." Their own alternative: a shoreline or channel lines animals up. One bedding surface; the maker is read from foot shape and size ("comparable to *Achillobator*"); no body fossil of the maker is known from those beds. Group travel is not pack hunting: "Whether this behavior was specifically hunting cannot be ascertained." Xing 2018 is a second site: four small trackways, but two cross, and the two-toed reading rests partly on a missing toe trace. Roach & Brinkman's alternative for Mount Tom (last build): animals walking a shoreline toward a carcass, with four trackways going the other way. Li's answer is the spacing: regular and closer than a stride, which a shoreline doesn't produce.
- [maker] The reconstruction and the tracks are shown together: the animal is inferred from the prints. Tracks don't come labeled. Xing 2024 calls *Fujianipus* a troodontid from one toe ratio, then sizes it with a troodontid leg ratio (197 cm hip, "likely an overestimation"); the dromaeosaurid ratio gives 156 cm. The obvious alternative maker, *Achillobator*, is excluded by assuming a foot nobody has found. Tsukiji's chip: the same trackway is a walk at 6.1 or 4.6 km/h depending on which clade you assume, and for other trackways the gait itself changes.
- [pack] First build: the two predictions as living animals. Logic of the test: pack-hunting mammals' young eat what the adults eat; asocial reptiles' young eat smaller prey. Crocodiles are the control and shift with size "like their modern counterparts." Ten teeth per bin used up 28-29% of the identified material, so more samples aren't coming soon. My copy is an uncorrected proof: the abstract and results give different means for medium crocodile teeth. The last quote is about a different animal: the *Utahraptor* block with an adult, a juvenile and a baby. It argues against Komodo-style solitude, not for a mammal-style pack.
- [display] (Now in Feathers, after feather stages.) The tail-fan argument rests on muscle models plus fans known in two early taxa (*Caudipteryx*, *Similicaudipteryx*). Persons: tails "may prove to be sexually dimorphic," untestable for now. Clark's sex call for *Plumadraco* is analogy to living birds; the specimen has no sex indicator of its own.
- [display] Li 2012's "iridescent" is a classifier call: 82% accurate on living birds, 58-100% for the fossil samples, no hue because keratin is gone. The narrow tail fan weakens earlier lift models.
- [diet] Fan's own words on gut contents and pellets: "a single data point," "the troodontid origin of these suspected gastric pellets cannot be confirmed." Each box in Fig 7 rests on a different amount of material; the isotope box is N = 5 individuals (Cullen & Cousens 2024). The predation traits are the skeleton's; the plant evidence is mostly indirect.

**Reproduction**
- [nests] Clark: four oviraptorids on nests among 17 Djadokhta adults, 23.5%, against none in that posture otherwise; "Other oviraptorid skeletons from the structureless sandstones ... are not in this position." Not laying: no eggs in the pelvis, though the pelvis is incomplete. Their limit, which Bi's isotopes later addressed: sitting on a nest is not the same as heating it, "an untestable assumption." *Nemegtomaia* (Fanti 2012) adds a second lineage, but insects ate at the body before final burial and there are no embryos or complete eggs. The egg-thief story: Osborn named *Oviraptor* as a nest robber "although there was no evidence to indicate predation."
- [brood] Bi 2021 is the first adult on a clutch with embryos inside. Embryos are late-stage, so "the adult clearly had tended the nest for an extended period and did not perish in the act of egg laying." No medullary bone, which "raises the possibility that the attending individual may have been male."
- [clutch] The giant-clutch result rests on three *Macroelongatoolithus* clutches, and body masses come from a regression of clutch volume on living birds and crocodiles. Tanaka's own caveat: in giants brooding "may have been less effective ... because there may have been less contact with the eggs."
- [care] Living animals before the fossil argument. Crocodile mothers guard but don't sit; ostriches share incubation (female by day, male by night); rhea males incubate alone. The ostrich is the contested point on the next slide: Varricchio 2008 coded it paternal, Birchard 2013 biparental.
- [parent] Same kind of plot, opposite reading. Varricchio: 433 living archosaurs, three dinosaur points, best fit paternal (corrected Akaike weight 0.698 after a 2009 erratum for base-10 logs). Plus no medullary bone in eight clutch-associated adults. Birchard: 1254 birds; ostrich recoded as biparental, megapodes dropped, average not maximum clutch; maternal and paternal lines then coincide, and the real split is precocial vs altricial. In the one order with all three care types, paternal clutches are the smallest. Ask: is the paternal line a care line or a ratite line? Then Jin 2020 (female with eggs inside, no medullary bone) removes the histology leg.
- [open] Three kinds of evidence agree: pores (Tanaka 2015), pigment (Wiemann 2017) and clutch geometry (Hogan & Varricchio 2023) all point to eggs partly buried, partly open. Strengths differ: Tanaka's phylogenetic method gives 0.513 / 0.487 for oviraptorosaurs, a coin toss; Wiemann's measured color is olive-green, "blue-green" only after a taphonomic correction, on three eggshells; Hogan's "indirect contact incubation" step rests on one lab experiment. Agreement among weak lines is still worth more than any one of them.
- [warm] Two methods on eggs, and they don't line up. Amiot: 35-40 °C, bird-like, but from 2 clean fits out of 7 eggs (four above 45 °C set aside, a third fits only if water loss is cut by two thirds); the model gives 44-56 days. Varricchio: one *Troodon* embryo tooth, 31 lines counted plus 8 estimated, converted with crocodile timing, gives 74 days, between reptile and bird. Different taxa (oviraptorosaurs vs *Troodon*), and Varricchio's own explanation is burial. Bi 2021 used Amiot's method with Amiot as co-author, so Bi's temperatures are the same line, not a second one.
- [hatch] Same observation (embryos at different stages), opposite mechanism. Bi: upper eggs nearer the adult got more heat. Yang: no contact incubation, so the gap comes from laying in rings. Yang's exclusion cites their own companion paper (Yang et al. 2019, "Reconstruction of oviraptorid clutches illuminates their unique nesting ..."), not in the folder. Both name the same alternative: some embryos died earlier.
- [repro-matrix] "Adult on the eggs" now has several specimens across genera, but none of the older ones has embryos; Bi's is the one that ties sitting to developed eggs. Columns with one entry versus three. "Eggs warmed" is where the lines collide; "Hatching" rests on one clutch of three eggs (Yang) and one of 24 (Bi).
- Dropped as separate slides: eggs inside (Jin 2020; its point, no medullary bone in a female with eggs, belongs under [parent]), embryos (Varricchio 2002; the egg-identity story can be told aloud), five stages kept at level 2.

**Feathers**
- [fstages] The sequence (filaments, branched, vaned, asymmetric) is agreed; where it starts isn't. The two dates differ by tens of millions of years because the answer depends on whether pterosaur and ornithischian filaments are feathers, and the deciding animals have no skin preserved. *Psittacosaurus*: hundreds of specimens, one with filaments; absence is weak evidence.
- [display] Three papers, three body parts, one answer. Li 2012: *Microraptor*'s tail fan is narrow, with two long midline feathers; "iridescent" is a classifier call (82% accurate on living birds, 58-100% for the fossil samples). Persons 2014: oviraptorosaur tails built for fanning, from muscle models plus fans in two early taxa. Foth 2014: long foot feathers arose three separate times on their tree, so not built for flight; display is the leftover function. All three argue display partly by elimination; none can test sex differences on the fossils they have.
- [wings] Two animals with vaned arm feathers that couldn't fly. Talori's robot *Caudipteryx* got lift "~8% of the body weight" at 8 m/s, flapping not modelled. *Zhenyuanlong*: short arms, big wings; Lü & Brusatte favor retention from flying ancestors over new display. Dececchi 2016: wing muscle set at 10% of body mass, generous, so non-paravian failures are strong failures; "display or brooding" is what is left.
- [asym] The disagreement: Xu 2017 finds an asymmetric feather (one tail feather) on *Jianianhualong*, a troodontid nobody thinks flew, so asymmetry isn't proof of flight. Kiat & O'Connor, calibrating on 346 living birds, find asymmetry the better flight predictor. Both can be right: asymmetry may persist briefly after flight is lost, and Kiat measured primaries, not tail feathers. Ask which feather each paper measured.

**Flight**
- [membrane] *Yi* fossil and its restoration together, then *Ambopteryx* (Wang et al. 2019, *Nature*; press restoration only, the paper isn't in the folder). Dececchi 2020 did not see the striations reported for *Yi*, saw feather filaments across the patches instead. The wing model they favor on anatomy fails as a glider; the models that pass (bat, pterosaur) are the ones the specimen least supports.
- [models] Two physics models agree on the robust part: nothing before Paraves meets the thresholds (Dececchi), and many paravians come close (Pei). Both rest on assumptions on the slide: wing muscle at 10% of body mass, "likely significant overestimations for non-paravians"; a maximum lift coefficient of 1.6. Some of Pei's passes need anaerobic muscle.
- [anatomy] Two anatomical lines agree that anchiornithines did not fly: feather asymmetry (Kiat) and arm length (Hartman: *Eosinopteryx* 0.78, *Anchiornis* 0.96-1.04, near or above the 0.70 line but short of *Archaeopteryx*). That conflicts with Pei, whose wing loading passes them. Kiat & O'Connor on Pei: their results "place this taxon [*Anchiornis*] on the cut off." Arm length is one number per specimen and can't see feathers.
- [origins] One tree, four readings. Pei's own data give two equally short answers: ≥3 origins, or one origin at Paraves with 6-11 losses ("the trees obtained have the exact same length"); parsimony can't choose, and the choice rests on whether gaining or losing flight is easier. Kiat & O'Connor put one origin below Pennaraptora, from nine primaries in *Caudipteryx* (could also be the ancestral count of a wing that never flew). Hartman: parallel experiments, bird flight late. Same fossils, each paper weighting a different trait. Have students move the marks in their heads: what fossil would decide it?
- [arch] Voeten: "avian powered flight must have originated before the latest Jurassic." Not a modern stroke: no keel, no supracoracoideus pulley.
- [shoulder] Wu 2024 is a review of drawings, no new analysis.
- [flight-matrix] *Archaeopteryx* is where every line has an entry and they agree. Anchiornithines are where the lines split: Pei passes them, Kiat and Hartman don't.

**Brains and the origin of birds**
- [brain] Volume and shape tell different stories. Balanoff: bird-like brain size "evolved multiple times," and *Archaeopteryx* is no outlier ("Endocranial volumes alone, of course, do not tell the whole story"). Torres: shape changed in stages, the last at crown birds; *Ichthyornis* "had an Archaeopteryx-like brain shape" (its endocast is crushed side to side). The wulst Balanoff saw in *Archaeopteryx* was "later shown to be a taphonomic artefact": one line withdrawn.
- [shrink] Lee and Torres agree the line shrank. Lee: tetanuran ~163 kg to bird ancestor ~0.8 kg, from femur length alone. Torres: the bird ancestor is 10.9 kg with only *Archaeopteryx* as a stem bird, 2.4 kg with every stem bird: the ancestral state is a product of the sample.
- [rates] Same branch, opposite answers, because "rate" means different things. Brusatte counts discrete character changes anywhere in the skeleton: fast. Wang & Zhou measure six limb-bone lengths: slow. A wing can lock its proportions while other parts change fast. Brusatte: "A Mesozoic naturalist would make no immediate distinction between a Velociraptor-type animal and an Archaeopteryx-type animal."
- [bias] (Level 2.) Lee names oversampling for the bird stem and gives three reasons the fast rates are "at least partly real"; Brusatte uses the same worry to set aside fast tyrannosaur rates while keeping fast bird rates.

**Synthesis**
- [matrix] Closest to birds is now skeletons and trees only. Flight has four kinds of evidence; the physics models and the tracks each answer only one question. Brooding has the most lines but most are one or a few specimens. Groups and diet rest on the fewest kinds, though tracks are a kind no other row has.
- [yours] Students fill this in for their own question.
