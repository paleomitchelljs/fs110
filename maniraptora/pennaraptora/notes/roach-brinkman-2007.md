# Roach & Brinkman 2007, "A reevaluation of cooperative pack hunting and gregariousness in *Deinonychus antirrhopus* and other nonavian theropod dinosaurs"

Roach & Brinkman. *Bulletin of the Peabody Museum of Natural History* 48(1):103-138 (2007).

File: `papers/Roach-Brinkman2007.pdf`, 36 pages. Read the abstract, introduction, phylogenetic-inference section, the Komodo ("ora") section, the YPM 64-75 reassessment, the trackway section and the conclusions; the bird and crocodile behaviour reviews skimmed. All figures viewed.

## Claim

- Target: Ostrom's wolf-pack *Deinonychus*, which "was based solely on his interpretation of the D. antirrhopus type locality, Yale Peabody Museum site 64-75": four *Deinonychus* (Maxwell 1999) with one subadult *Tenontosaurus*.
- Method: phylogenetic bracketing with living diapsids (lizards, crocodiles, birds), the same approach used for brooding and sleeping postures.
- The null: "the null hypothesis should therefore be that nonavian theropod dinosaurs were solitary hunters or, at most, foraged in loose associations." "mammal-like pack hunting is unknown in extant diapsids, many do have gregarious behaviors." Bird exceptions (black vultures on skunks, golden eagles in hard winters) are rare, kin-based and recent.
- Their admitted limit: "evolution often behaves in nonparsimonious ways," and they "refrain here from actually quantifying" the likelihood argument.
- Analogue: the Komodo dragon. Oras gather at kills by scent ("an average of 4, and as many as 17"), fight, and eat smaller oras; juveniles keep away. Prediction for *Deinonychus*: "Such resource partitioning in D. antirrhopus could account for the absence of the remains of small juvenile D. antirrhopus individuals at both the YPM 64-75 and OMNH V706 sites."
- New evidence: a *Deinonychus* hand claw lodged among the chevrons of another's tail (YPM 5203), read as combat; a healed broken toe (YPM 5205). At OMNH V706, a lone, scattered *Deinonychus* among *Tenontosaurus* is read as a cannibalized scavenger.
- Trackways: parallel theropod trackways (Mount Tom, Ostrom 1972) may be animals following a shoreline toward a carcass: "most of the trackmakers there may have been walking parallel to the paleoshoreline"; four trackways run the other way.

Strong as a null and as a source of testable predictions: Frederickson 2020 tested the diet one (young eating different prey, as in reptiles), and Li 2008 answered the trackway one with regular, sub-stride spacing. Weak points: the phylogenetic bracket includes birds, whose group behaviour is varied; the hand-claw-in-tail could be post-mortem mixing in a scattered quarry, which they concede makes bones hard to assign to individuals.

Pairs with [Frederickson et al. 2020](frederickson-2020.md) and [Li et al. 2008](li-2008.md).
