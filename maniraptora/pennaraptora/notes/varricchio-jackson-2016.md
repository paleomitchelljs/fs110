# Varricchio & Jackson 2016, "Reproduction in Mesozoic birds and evolution of the modern avian reproductive mode"

Varricchio & Jackson. *The Auk* 133(4):654-684 (2016). doi 10.1642/AUK-15-216.1. Review.

- `papers/varricchio2016.pdf`, 32 pages, the BioOne download with a cover page. Same text; one can be deleted.

Read the body in full (the appendix specimen tables only skimmed); all seven figures viewed.

## Claim

- Living birds share a "modern avian reproductive mode" (Table 1): exposed eggs, brooding, one ovary and oviduct, one egg a day, egg turning, chalazae (the cords that hold the yolk), relatively large asymmetric eggs, precocial young primitively, mostly biparental care.
- Two directions of study: ornithologists work "from the extant crown clades down the tree"; paleontologists using sister taxa "have worked up the tree toward the primitive condition of Aves." Ornithologists' models of parental care evolution: "None of the 3 more extensive models ... conform to the dinosaur fossil record."
- Oviraptors and troodontids share with birds "sequential ovulation, relatively larger eggs, various aspects of eggshell microstructure, some degree of parental care (possibly paternal), at least some adult–egg contact, and precocial hatchlings." They differ in "2 functional reproductive tracts, smaller than expected relative egg size, elongate egg-shape, and eggs still largely buried." Buried eggs "would likely preclude egg rotation and, thus, may have lacked chalazae."
- Troodontids are closer to birds than oviraptors in eggs: more asymmetric, unornamented, possibly three shell layers, avian porosity. "The tighter clutch configuration, greater exposure of eggs, and avian levels of porosity favor contact incubation in troodontids."
- Clutches are ~3 times larger than expected for a bird or reptile of the same adult mass; clutch scaling best fits paternal care.
- Mesozoic bird eggs are almost all enantiornithine. Eggs 26-70 mm, elongation 1.6-2.2, between non-avian theropods (2.0-3.0) and modern birds (~1.4). Egg mass 49-75% of a modern bird of the same size. Enantiornithine eggs sit "planted upright within sediments," singly or in clutches, often in colonies, with "superprecocial young" (Khermeen Tsav embryos possibly flight-capable on hatching).
- The proposed ovarian follicles in Jehol birds (one left ovary) are questioned: "Lacking a demonstrable preservation mechanism, the proposed ovarian follicles in the Liaoning birds remain questionable."
- Five stages (Fig 7): (1) pre-maniraptoran theropods; (2) oviraptor-grade maniraptorans (larger, elongate eggs; paired laying; partial burial with attendant adult; paternal care); (3) troodontid-grade paravians (unornamented, asymmetric, planted eggs; avian porosity; tighter clutches); (4) Enantiornithes (one functioning oviduct; single eggs or clutches fully or partly buried); (5) basal Neornithes (larger eggs, sediment-free nests, egg turning, chalazae).
- Extinction: "Of all Mesozoic Dinosauria, only the Neornithes, the sole surviving K–Pg clade, apparently possessed chalazae and nested completely free of sediment." Sediment-free nesting lets birds nest in trees, on ledges, in cavities, and move eggs if threatened.

## Push-back

- The K–Pg link is a correlation with one surviving clade; the authors present it as "may have played a crucial role," not a test.
- Several key inputs are the authors' own earlier claims (egg pairing in *Troodon*, paternal care from clutch scaling). They answer Grellet-Tinner et al.'s criticism of pairing directly; worth letting students see that this is an argument between groups.
- The stage 4 enantiornithine picture rests on Mongolian, Argentine and Romanian sites; there is "no information" for basal ornithuromorphs.

Pairs with [Varricchio et al. 2002](varricchio-2002.md) (the *Troodon* clutch), [Hogan & Varricchio 2023](hogan-2023.md) (partial burial as a step) and [Tanaka et al. 2015](tanaka-2015.md) (porosity).

- **Fig 2 (p5)**, six color photos: a duck nest (A), the Polish expedition's 1970 oviraptor clutch ring in the field (B), the *Troodon* nesting trace in plaster and in the ground (C, D), *Troodon* clutch MOR 963 with steep eggs (E), and *Confuciusornis* with long tail feathers (F). (A) vs (B) shows the egg-shape and layout contrast at once; (E) is a good *Troodon* clutch photo. 300 ppi; crops well per panel.
- **Fig 3 (p8)**, eggshell sections in a row: hen SEM, *Troodon* SEM and thin section, enantiornithine, *Triprismatoolithus*. Layer bars marked. Sharp but specialist.
- **Fig 4 (p15)**, five enantiornithine eggs photographed on black: three Mongolian ootaxa and two Neuquén eggs. Clear, shows small, mildly asymmetric eggs. Usable.
- **Fig 5 (p16)**, two scatterplots (egg diameter vs length; shell thickness vs length). Plain.
- **Fig 6 (p16)**, (A) ground surface at Neuquén with arrows at five round egg cross-sections (upright eggs seen from above); (B) *Styloolithus* clutch with steep eggs and a bird limb bone on top. (A) is hard to read without the arrows; (B) is a good image of an enantiornithine sitting on eggs.
- **Fig 7 (p21)**, cladogram with five numbered stage circles and colored drawings at the tips: oviraptor and troodontid on part-buried eggs, enantiornithine, bird on an open nest, with male/female symbols for care. The summary slide for the paper; crop with or without the long caption.
