# Lee et al. 2014, "Sustained miniaturization and anatomical innovation in the dinosaurian ancestors of birds"

Lee, Cau, Naish & Dyke. *Science* 345:562-566 (2014). doi 10.1126/science.1252243.

File: `papers/Leeetal.2014sustainedminiaturizationandanatomicalinnovationinthedinosaurianancestryofbirds.pdf`, 6 pages. The first page is the end of an unrelated *Science* paper on cultural history (Schich et al.); the Lee paper starts mid-page 2. Read in full; all three figures viewed.

## Claim

- Data: two matrices. Data set 1: 120 taxa × 1549 characters, "including autapomorphies and invariant characters"; data set 2: 100 taxa × 421 characters (built for alvarezsaurs). Femur length as the size proxy, used only up to Avialae.
- Method: Bayesian (BEAST, adapted from virus epidemiology) estimating tree, dates, rates and ancestral sizes together, rather than "inferring topology first, then sometimes inferring divergence dates, and then optimizing the trait of interest."
- Size result: no trend across all theropods, but along the bird stem "a prolonged, directional trend in size reduction that spans at least 50 million years," with "rapid decreases in 12 consecutive branches from Tetanurae onward." Ancestral sizes: tetanuran ~198 Ma, ~163 kg; coelurosaur ~27 kg; maniraptoran ~10 kg; paravian ~3 kg; bird ~0.8 kg.
- Why the trend appears: "the oldest representatives of successively closer outgroups to birds tend to be progressively smaller" (*Sinraptor* ~900-1600 kg, *Guanlong* ~100 kg, *Haplocheirus* ~17 kg, *Caudipteryx* ~5 kg, *Jinfengopteryx*/*Archaeopteryx* ~0.5 kg).
- Rate result: every bird-stem branch from Neotetanurae onward evolves "at least twice as fast as the average theropod 'background' rate," on average about four times (3.19% vs 0.88% per lineage per million years).
- Interpretation: shrinking "would have facilitated the evolution of many novelties associated with small body size, such as reorientation of body mass, increased aerial ability, and paedomorphic skulls with reduced snouts but enlarged eyes and brains."
- They name their main bias: "Rates of innovation along the bird stem are potentially inflated by oversampling of characters on this lineage (by avian-centric researchers)," and give three reasons it is "at least partly real."

## Push-back and pairing

- Disagrees in emphasis with [Brusatte et al. 2014](brusatte-2014.md): Lee places fast change early and sustained across the stem (peak between Neotetanurae and Paraves); Brusatte places the rate shift at Avialae itself after a slow assembly. Both acknowledge "different types of data will pinpoint changes at slightly different positions."
- The size trend depends on the oldest member of each outgroup being basal and representative of its ancestor; a single large or small early fossil moves the curve.
- Femur length → mass is a strong regression (r > 0.995) within non-avian theropods, which is why they stop at Avialae.
- Links to [Balanoff et al. 2013](balanoff-2013.md): they argue brain expansion was not only a side-effect of shrinking bodies; Lee suggests shrinking helped. Compatible but different emphasis.
