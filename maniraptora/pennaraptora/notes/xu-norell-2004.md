# Xu & Norell 2004, "A new troodontid dinosaur from China with avian-like sleeping posture"

Xu & Norell. *Nature* 431:838-841, 14 Oct 2004. doi 10.1038/nature02898.

File: `papers/xu2004.pdf`, 4 pages (starts and ends with neighbouring Nature letters). Read in full; both figures viewed. Follow-up: [Gao et al. 2012](gao-2012.md), the second *Mei* specimen.

## Claim

- *Mei long*, IVPP V12733, Lujiatun beds, lowest Yixian Fm., "older than 128 and younger than 139 million years." ~53 cm. "not an adult ... However, the holotype is also not a hatchling."
- Name: "Mei from Chinese, meaning to sleep soundly; long from Chinese, meaning dragon."
- Posture: "The body sits on folded symmetrical hindlimbs. The forelimbs are also symmetrical and extend laterally, folded avian-like next to the body ... The neck curves posteriorly on the left side of the body so that part of the head lies medial to the left elbow at the side of the trunk. This posture is identical to the stereotypical 'tuck-in' sleeping posture of many living birds."
- Why life posture, not death pose: Lujiatun beds are "instant catastrophic mass mortality events preserved in tuffaceous ashes up to 3 m in thickness"; other Yixian dinosaurs show "typical death poses with heads bent back above the spine and the hind appendages and tails extended."
- Inference: "the tuck-in posture reduces surface area and conserves heat in the head ... the physiological/thermal implications of this and other fossil evidence (for example, brooding and feathers) are highly suggestive that these animals shared a homeothermic physiology with modern avians." They hedge: "Although we are unable to measure it directly."
- Anatomy: sister to *Sinovenator* at the base of Troodontidae. Small skull, very long hindlimbs, "extremely large nares extending posteriorly over one-half of the maxillary tooth row," no jugal-postorbital contact, suggesting "some kind of cranial kinesis (probably prokinesis) evolved in the early evolution of eumaniraptorans."

The step from posture to homeothermy is the weakest link: the tuck reduces heat loss in living birds, but resting tucked doesn't require endothermy. The authors call it "highly suggestive," not shown.
