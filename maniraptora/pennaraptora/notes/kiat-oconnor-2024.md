# Kiat & O'Connor 2024, "Functional constraints on the number and shape of flight feathers"

Kiat & O'Connor. *PNAS* 121(8):e2306639121 (2024). doi 10.1073/pnas.2306639121. Open access (PMC10895369, downloaded from the PMC open-data bucket).

File: `papers/kiat-oconnor2024.pdf`, 11 pages. Main text read in full; all six figures viewed.

## Claim

- Question: most flight studies of fossils use bones and wing area; few use the flight feathers themselves. Do feather number and shape track flight in living birds, and what do they say about fossils?
- Data: living birds from all orders (n = 346 taxa; 275 volant, 22 flightless in one model), plus 66 fossil specimens of 35 Mesozoic taxa. Traits: number of primaries (hand feathers), secondaries (forearm), tail-feather pairs, and primary vane asymmetry.
- Living birds: "all volant neornithines have between nine and eleven primary feathers." Primary count changes slowly after flight is lost: it shifted in every lineage flightless for more than 35 million years and is kept in recent losses (kākāpō, flightless cormorant). Vane asymmetry changes faster, so it "is a more reliable predictor of volant ability in fossil taxa."
- Two kinds of trait: slow ones (number) "reflect the ancestral condition," fast ones (asymmetry) "reflect current locomotor function." Used "in tandem."
- Fossils: "Mesozoic birds and Microraptor have remex morphologies consistent with extant volant birds." Anchiornithines (*Anchiornis*, *Serikornis*, *Eosinopteryx*) "deviate significantly providing strong evidence this clade was not volant": low asymmetry and more primaries than living fliers.
- *Caudipteryx*: nine primaries "suggests a volant ancestor," but short arms and long legs "preclude flight," so it was secondarily flightless.
- Big conclusion: "volant abilities are plesiomorphic to Pennaraptora," a "single origin of dinosaurian flight," and "the early stages of feathered wing evolution are not sampled by the currently available fossil record."
- Hedge in the discussion: "we refrain from making conclusive statements regarding this controversy but rather present important new results that complicate and challenge the current interpretations."
- On [Pei et al. 2020](pei-2020.md): their results "place this taxon [*Anchiornis*] on the cut off between powered flight potential and flightlessness."
- On Feo et al. 2015 (asymmetry doesn't separate fliers from flightless birds): the difference is "most likely" sampling; Feo's flightless birds mostly lost flight recently.

## Push-back

- The single-origin conclusion depends on reading nine primaries in *Caudipteryx* as inherited from a flying ancestor. The same count could be the ancestral state of a feathered arm that never flew; living birds can't separate those, since all of them descend from fliers. The calibration set has no animals that are "pre-flight."
- "Volant" here includes gliding. That widens what "single origin of flight" means relative to Pei's "powered flight potential."
- Asymmetry ratios in fossils depend on preservation and on which feather is measured; samples per fossil taxon are small (several taxa = 1 specimen).

## Interactions to show

This paper and [Pei et al. 2020](pei-2020.md) use the same fossils and reach opposite pictures:
- Pei (bones + wing area, on a tree): powered flight ≥3 times; anchiornithines pass wing loading.
- Kiat & O'Connor (feathers, calibrated on living birds): one origin, many losses; anchiornithines flightless.
And Pei's own supplementary test found single origin + 6-11 losses costs the same tree length. A clean three-way slide.
Also contrasts with [Dececchi et al. 2016](dececchi-2016.md) on *Caudipteryx*: never flew vs lost flight. Same animal, opposite history, both consistent with "doesn't fly now."
