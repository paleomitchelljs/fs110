# Gimsa, Sleigh & Gimsa 2016, "The riddle of Spinosaurus aegyptiacus' dorsal sail"

*Geological Magazine* 153(3):544-547. DOI 10.1017/S0016756815000801. CC-BY. First online 17 Nov 2015, so [Hone & Holtz 2017](hone-holtz-2017.md) cites it as "Gimsa et al., 2015." File: `spinos/papers/the-riddle-of-spinosaurus-aegyptiacus-dorsal-sail.pdf`, 4 pages. PDF page = journal page minus 543. Rapid Communication. Fig 1 viewed (PDF p3).

## Claim

The sail was submerged in use, like a sailfish dorsal fin. It "would have improved manoeuvrability and provided the hydrodynamic fulcrum for powerful neck and tail movements such as those made by sailfish or thresher sharks when stunning or injuring prey," and "could have been employed as a screen for encircling prey underwater."

## Facts and quotes

No measurement, model or test. Reasoning only.

- Author affiliations: Chair for Biophysics, University of Rostock; Sleigh Technical Translations, Berlin; Institute of Behavioural Physiology, Leibniz Institute for Farm Animal Biology.
- Motivation: "This manuscript was partly motivated by discussions with colleagues... on the importance of biophysics in the education of biology students."
- Starting argument: "Spinosaurus' semiaquatic life would always have risked submerging the sail by accident." "an insufficiently hydrodynamically stable sail was not an option." So: "the dorsal sail of Spinosaurus had to be submergible."
- Sail shape: "almost rectangular, unlike the rounded, arc-shaped sails of related non-aquatic spinosaurids."
- Sailfish: "hydrodynamically speaking the sail was homologous to the dorsal fins of sailfish." They mean analogous. Teaching point: sailfish and dinosaur share no homologous sail, and the paper's own keywords pair "convergent evolution" with "homologous organs."
- Sailfish difference they admit: "sailfish can fully withdraw their dorsal and long pectoral fins." Their reasoning doesn't resolve that the sail can't fold. [Hone & Holtz 2021](hone-holtz-2021.md): "would not function in the manner of sailfish," since the fish sail "is both flexible, collapsable and mediolaterally thin." [Sereno 2022](sereno-2022.md): "No primary or secondary vertebrate swimmer has a comparable drag-magnifying, rigid dorsal sail including sailfish."
- Tail: "resembled that of the thresher shark, employed to slap the water." Written before the deep tail of [Ibrahim 2020](ibrahim-2020.md).
- Shock absorption: neural spine "proximal flanges... may have had a similar structure and an analogous function" to the oil-filled ethmoid at the base of a swordfish bill. "Here we hypothesize." No data.
- Group hunting: "this efficiency could have been further increased when hunting in groups."
- Assumes the conclusion it tests: "lead us to assume that Spinosaurus was both a deep-water hunter and an excellent swimmer."
- Diving: "an animal with twice the characteristic length of a geometrically similar animal could submerge for around twice as long," "it is tempting to assume that Spinosaurus took advantage of the potentials of a submerged animal of 10 tonnes." Compare buoyancy in [Henderson 2018](henderson-2018.md) and [Sereno 2022](sereno-2022.md), both of which find it can't submerge easily. 10 tonnes vs 6,500 and 7,390 kg in those.
- Close: "To restrict a ferocious hunter such as Spinosaurus to the surface, with its sail raised in the air for display, would be unsatisfactory for a starving animal and a hypothetical observer of the scene." An aesthetic argument, stated openly.
- They ask for the test: "Future numerical simulations... hold promise for determining that the main function of the dorsal sail was hydrodynamic." Sereno 2022 later runs drag: sail adds 33.4%.
- Reception in the folder: "A very speculative paper" (Henderson 2018). "not here considered a viable hypothesis" (Hone & Holtz 2017). "seems unlikely" (Hone & Holtz 2021).

## Figures

**Fig 1 (PDF p3): OUTLINES, not skeletons.** Three body outlines. "Circumferences were drawn in line with the skeleton published in Ibrahim et al. (2014), and averaged from images in the internet."
- a: *Spinosaurus* "in resting or stalking posture," dashed waterline at the head and sail top, legs hanging.
- b: "Sailfish with sail raised."
- c: *Spinosaurus* "swimming submerged," hind leg "in stroke position," and tail "with hypothetical crocodile-like horny scales."
- Specimens: none directly. The *Spinosaurus* outline comes from Ibrahim 2014's composite (Fig 2A). So inherits FSAC-KK 11888 + referred + Stromer's drawings, and the old thin tail. "averaged from images in the internet" is their wording and applies to the outlines in general.
- Good visual pairing even though it's speculative: the eye sees the sail and fish fin match, and that is all the evidence on offer.
