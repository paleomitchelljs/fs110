# Abelisaur papers: key claims and figure descriptions

Prepared for a lecture deck modeled on `therizinos/`. Papers live in `abelisaurs/papers/` (gitignored). Files are named year-firstauthor-taxon.

**How these notes were made.** Claims and numbers come from the text of each PDF. Figure descriptions come from looking at the rendered page, not from the captions. I looked at 3 to 5 key figures per paper, not every panel: the long ones (Pol 2024, 23 figures; Baiano 2023, 34 figures; Langer 2019, 13 figures) are covered by their most informative figures only. Where I describe a figure, I name its number. Where a figure was not viewed, I say so.

**Two things to know before using the PDFs**
- `2003-rogers-majungatholus.pdf` (Rogers et al. 2003) starts with the last page of an unrelated oceanography paper (a Methods section and references on internal-wave mixing, Nature 422:514). The Majungatholus article begins on the second half of that PDF page and runs to p. 518. The PDF's embedded title ("66346 495..499") is wrong.
- `2025-velandia-vitosaura.pdf` is Velandia et al. 2025, the newest paper in the folder (*Vitosaura*).

## 1. Grillo & Delcourt 2017: "Allometry and body length of abelisauroid theropods: *Pycnonemosaurus nevesi* is the new king"

**Central claim.** Body length (BL) of abelisauroids can be estimated consistently from bone measurements, and the biggest one is *Pycnonemosaurus*.

**Key facts**
- Method: BL was re-estimated for the "seven most complete specimens" and compared against 40 skull, vertebral and limb measurements using bivariate regressions. That allowed estimates for 30 more specimens (37 total; 8 basal abelisauroids and noasaurids, 27 abelisaurids, 3 *incertae sedis*).
- "Strong correlations (R² > 0.96)" for all vertebrae and hindlimb measurements, plus skull height, skull-roof length, lacrimal-squamosal, scapulocoracoid and humerus. Most skull and forelimb measurements correlate weakly, which the authors blame on "extreme morphological transformations" in abelisaurids.
- Sizes: *Pycnonemosaurus* 8.9 ± 0.3 m; *Carnotaurus* 7.8 ± 0.3; *Abelisaurus* 7.4 ± 0.7; *Ekrixinatosaurus* 7.4 ± 0.8. Smallest: *Velocisaurus* 1.5 ± 0.1 m (noasaurid) and *Genusaurus* 3.6 m (abelisaurid).
- Size through time: 3.3 ± 2.5 m (basal abelisauroids and Noasauridae), 5.4 ± 1.8 m (basal Brachyrostra and Majungasauridae), 7.1 ± 2.1 m (Furileusaura). Within any region and epoch, BL "usually varied from 4 to 8 m."
- Skull measurements scale negatively; skull height scales almost isometrically while skull-roof length scales more negatively. The authors suggest this bends the skull and may explain the upturned snout in large taxa. (This is a hypothesis, not a test.)
- *Pycnonemosaurus* was previously proposed at 7–8 m; the new estimate is about 1.4 m longer.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (anterior caudal vertebrae).* A grid of 24 panels on a light gray ruled background with a 0–800 mm scale bar. Top row is lateral views (A1–L1), bottom row is cranial views (A2–L2). Panels A–F are the six caudals of *Pycnonemosaurus*: big, knobby, pale-brown fossil blocks, each almost as wide as it is tall. G–L are cut-out drawings of other taxa (*Carnotaurus*, *Ekrixinatosaurus*, *Aucasaurus*, etc.) that are smaller and spindlier, with thin neural spines. The size contrast is visible before you read a label. Strong figure for a "look at the bones" opening.
- *Fig. 3 (skeleton reconstructions).* Six stacked skeletal silhouettes (A–F), largest at top, in white bones over a black body outline, on a metre ruler from 0 to 8. A dashed vertical line through the hips is the alignment reference. *Carnotaurus* at top, *Masiakasaurus* at the bottom, a fraction of the size. Clean and teachable as a size ladder.
- *Fig. 4 (skull measurements vs BL).* Three log-log scatterplots with filled and open symbols (triangles, circles, squares), solid and dashed regression lines, and labelled outliers (*Abelisaurus*, *Carnotaurus*) off the lines. Slope values (k = 0.28 to 1.12) are printed on each panel. Dense; needs a redraw for slides.
- *Fig. 7 (all 37 specimens).* Horizontal bar chart, largest at top (*Pycnonemosaurus*, about 9 m), smallest at bottom (*Ligabueino*, under 1 m). Light gray bars are the min–max range, dark gray bars the standard deviation, black ticks the BL from earlier literature. X-axis 0–11 m. The best figure in the paper for showing that the ranking is not just one number.
- *Fig. 8 (BL through evolution).* A: phylogeny on the left, bar chart on the right, with shaded boxes marking each clade's size range and the mean written beside it (3.3 ± 2.5, 3.6 ± 2.2, 4.5 ± 0.4, 5.3 ± 1.7, 5.4 ± 1.8, 6.4 ± 1.8, 7.1 ± 2.1 m). B: a globe at left with black leader lines to bar clusters, bars coloured by age (legend runs Jurassic to Maastrichtian) and grouped by region. Good use of colour, but the globe adds little.

**Assessment for the deck.** Easy claim-evidence-test fit: "which was the biggest?" is a prediction slide; the answer overturns the *Carnotaurus*-as-giant assumption. The uncertainty bars matter: *Pycnonemosaurus* ± 0.3 versus *Carnotaurus* ± 0.3 does not overlap, but *Abelisaurus* and *Ekrixinatosaurus* are within error of each other.

## 2. Longrich et al. 2017: "An abelisaurid from the latest Cretaceous (late Maastrichtian) of Morocco, North Africa"

**Central claim.** A new, large, short-jawed abelisaurid (*Chenanisaurus barbaricus*) shows that abelisaurids and titanosaurs persisted in mainland Africa until just before the end-Cretaceous extinction, and that it belongs to a lineage distinct from the South American, Indo-Malagasy and European ones.

**Key facts**
- Holotype OCP DEK-GE 772: a partial left dentary with three tooth crowns and four roots. Referred: isolated premaxillary teeth (OCP DEK-GE 457, 458) and a maxillary tooth.
- Locality: upper Maastrichtian phosphates (Couche III, Sidi Chennane mines, Ouled Abdoun Basin, Morocco).
- The jaw is "proportionally deep, indicating a proportionally short, deep mandible," bowed in lateral view like carnotaurines; ten alveoli.
- Estimated body length "may have reached 7–8 m, comparable to the largest known abelisaurids."
- Tooth OCP DEK-GE 457: 8.5 mesial denticles per 5 mm at the tip; distal 8 per mm at tip and mid-crown, 13 at the base.
- Phylogeny (PAUP*, implied weighting, K = 2; 430 characters including a continental-plate character; 50,000 trees): CI 0.5893, RI 0.7277. The Morocco animal sits in its own lineage; the result is "not well-supported." The authors argue that Gondwanan fragmentation produced endemic lineages in the Late Cretaceous.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 3 (holotype dentary).* Three views on a black background. A (lateral): a pale cream, pitted, deeply grooved bone block with brown tooth crowns peeking above the top edge; labelled grooves and foramina (gr, nvf, vas). B (medial): the interdental plates and symphysis. C (dorsal): the tooth row seen from above as a line of round sockets, with a 10 cm bar. The photography is excellent; it looks like a museum plate.
- *Fig. 7 (tooth).* Two close-ups: A, a tall tan-brown tooth crown on black; B, the saw-edged carina magnified so the denticles read as a row of tiny teeth (10 mm and 1 mm scales).
- *Fig. 8 (jaw comparison).* Four outline jaws stacked: *Majungasaurus* (long, shallow, many teeth), *Carnotaurus* (shorter, deeper), *Ekrixinatosaurus* (deeper, bowed), *Chenanisaurus* (a short, very deep, bowed jaw, with only the preserved piece in white and the rest a gray guess). Scale 100 mm. Good for "look how much of this is reconstruction."
- *Fig. 9 (scale).* A silhouette lineup: a man leading a camel (1 m scale bar) at left, a dark gray *Chenanisaurus* in the middle, and a taller titanosaur behind it. The only decorative figure; the human and camel do the work.
- *Fig. 10 (phylogeny).* Tree standing on a geologic time column with a red K–Pg line near the top and a blue band marking "Gondwanan fragmentation" (Early Cretaceous); tips labelled by continent (AF, SA, EU, IN, MA).
- *Fig. 11 (map).* Late Cretaceous paleo-map with theropod silhouettes numbered 1–14 at each locality. *Chenanisaurus* is number 1.

**Assessment.** The claim rests on a jaw fragment and teeth, so the strongest teaching point is how much is inferred from how little. Fig. 8 shows that directly.

## 3. Hendrickx & Bell 2021: "The scaly skin of the abelisaurid *Carnotaurus sastrei*"

**Central claim.** The skin of *Carnotaurus* is a mosaic of large conical "feature scales" scattered randomly among small flat basement scales. It is the best skin known for any theropod, and the only scaly example outside Tetanurae.

**Key facts**
- Specimen MACN-CH 894, excavated in 1984 (Bonaparte's 8th expedition); skin impressions from the shoulder, thorax, tail and possibly the neck.
- Feature scales: conical, 20–65 mm across. Basement scales: below 14 mm, non-imbricating, separated by narrow interstitial tissue.
- Contrary to earlier descriptions, feature scales are "randomly distributed and neither form discrete rows nor show progressive variations in their size along parts of the body."
- Basement scales vary by region: small and elongated (thorax), large and polygonal (scapula), circular-to-lenticular (tail).
- Compared with more than 30 dinosaur taxa; 3D photogrammetry of the tail patch.
- Speculation: skin may have helped shed heat in an animal over 1,000 kg. The authors flag this as speculation.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (where the skin comes from).* A grey-green sculpted 3D model of *Carnotaurus* in profile, with photographs of the six skin blocks arranged around it, each linked to its body region by a dotted line. Blue dots on the body mark locations, a few with question marks. A human silhouette gives scale. This is the figure to use if you want students to see how little of the body is actually skin.
- *Fig. 5 (thoracic skin).* A large gray-brown rock slab with a black-and-white scale bar, dotted white arcs circling feature scales, a rib running through the block (thr), and axes (dorsal, posterior). B and C below are close-ups of single feature scales, each ringed by a dotted arc with an X at its apex. Photographed dead-on and well lit. The scales are subtle in the rock; the dotted lines are doing a lot of work.
- *Fig. 12 (scales across dinosaurs).* A 4 × 4 grid of 16 photos, one per row by group: *Carnotaurus* (A–D), non-tyrannosaurid tetanurans (E–H), tyrannosaurids (I–L), sauropods (M–P). Casts in different colours (green, yellow, brown, gray) make the grid easy to scan; a scale bar sits in each panel. Strong for a "which of these is not like the others" activity.
- Not viewed: Fig. 13 (log-plot of basement-scale length against body mass).

**Assessment.** A good example of a descriptive paper that overturns a claim (rows and size gradients) by looking again at the same specimen.

## 4. Tortosa et al. 2014: "A new abelisaurid dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous of southern France"

**Central claim.** *Arcovenator escotae*, from the late Campanian of the Aix-en-Provence Basin, is more closely related to Indian and Malagasy abelisaurids than to South American ones; so Europe and Africa may have played a larger role in abelisaurid dispersal than thought, "which apparently involved crossing marine barriers."

**Key facts**
- Holotype MHNA-PV-2011.12.1, a basicranium with associated postorbital; also a squamosal, tooth, tibia, fibula and anterior caudal vertebrae.
- Phylogeny: *Arcovenator* in Majungasaurinae with *Majungasaurus*, *Indosaurus*, *Rajasaurus* and *Rahiolisaurus*. *Genusaurus* and *Tarascosaurus* are identified as basal abelisaurids.
- Strict consensus of 20 MPTs, 753 steps, CI = 0.584, RI = 0.711.
- Authors recognise *Arcovenator*, *Genusaurus* and *Tarascosaurus* as distinct taxa and note a "general increase in body mass among French abelisaurids during the Late Cretaceous."

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (cranial anatomy).* Black-background photographs (a–e) of the braincase from several angles, bones pale gray with white labels (dozens of abbreviations), plus panel f, a skull reconstruction in which the known bone is white and the missing skull is a dark outline modelled on *Majungasaurus*. Excellent specimen photography; label-heavy.
- *Fig. 7 (tree on a time scale).* Strict consensus tree hung from a geologic time bar (Triassic–Cretaceous) with black bars for stratigraphic ranges, node numbers, and tiny continent silhouettes (Pangea, Africa, India, Madagascar, S. America) at key nodes. *Arcovenator* is in bold. Dense; the continent icons are the useful part.
- *Fig. 8 (France map and time).* A: map of the basin with shaded rock units (Upper Campanian–Maastrichtian continental in dark gray) and dashed faults, with locality numbers. Below: panels b–g stack a timescale (Ma, polarity chron, stage) above a row of theropod silhouettes sized by estimated body length, placed at their stratigraphic position (numbered 1–9). Reads as a timeline of French abelisaurids getting bigger.

**Assessment.** Biogeography is the main take. The claim ("crossing marine barriers") is an inference from tree topology, not from any direct evidence of dispersal; the paper does not test it.

## 5. Wang et al. 2017: "Extreme ontogenetic changes in a ceratosaurian theropod" (*Limusaurus*)

**Central claim.** *Limusaurus inextricabilis* is the only known reptile to lose its teeth and grow a beak after birth, and this extreme change has little effect on its phylogenetic placement.

**Key facts**
- 78 ontogenetically variable features identified in a growth series of six stages (I–VI; stages I–V used in most tests), based on skeletochronology and histology.
- Teeth: "stage I Limusaurus has at least 42 teeth, stage II has 34 teeth, and stage IV and V are toothless." CT shows the alveoli fill in and the jaw bone becomes a canal.
- Gastroliths absent in stages I and II, present in more mature individuals.
- Stable isotopes (δ¹³C and δ¹⁸O from apatite carbonate): immature individuals "display a wider range of isotope values"; the most mature plot with herbivores. Authors infer a shift from omnivory to herbivory.
- Phylogeny: scoring juvenile versus adult *Limusaurus* barely changes the tree; both stay in Noasauridae-or-near. Authors recommend cladistic assessment of juvenile taxa.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1 (six stages).* Left column: A–F, six small, fragile line drawings of skeletons from tiny to large, each paired with a rectangle of dark bone-section photograph with black arrows marking growth lines (LAGs). Right: G, two large black-and-white skeleton silhouettes (juvenile above, adult below) with numbered arrows (1–4) pointing at the features that change. Clean and clear; the size series reads at a glance.
- *Fig. 2 (jaw CT).* Black background. Left columns of CT renderings: teal bones with orange teeth (stage I), pink and purple versions in the middle column, yellow-orange tooth-and-canal renderings at right; stages go from top (toothed) to bottom (toothless). Right column F–H: schematic cylinders (blue = jaw, yellow band = canal, orange = teeth) showing the tooth row shrinking. Visually the best figure here.
- *Fig. 3 (isotopes).* Scatterplot of δ¹³C (x) against δ¹⁸O (y) with a pink polygon for carnivorous dinosaurs and a blue one for herbivores, a theropod, ornithischian and sauropod silhouette at each, and *Limusaurus* points (yellow open circles for stages I–III, black filled for IV–V) joined by a dashed line. The adults sit inside the herbivore field.
- *Fig. 4 (trees).* A: tree using subadult and adult scoring; B: the same with the juvenile; coloured node dots (Ceratosauridae red, Abelisauridae orange, Noasauridae green, Elaphrosaurinae purple) and asterisks. Hard to read at slide size.

**Assessment.** Best "claim / test" paper for a lecture on life history: a prediction slide ("which stage has teeth?"), then the CT figure.

## 6. Sereno, Wilson & Conrad 2004: "New dinosaurs link southern landmasses in the Mid-Cretaceous"

**Central claim.** New fossils from Niger put abelisauroids and their immediate antecedents on Africa through the Early to mid-Cretaceous, which is evidence of continued faunal exchange among Gondwanan landmasses until about 100 Myr ago. The authors propose a "pan-Gondwana" model against the "Africa-first" breakup model.

**Key facts**
- Three levels: an "immediate abelisauroid antecedent of Early Cretaceous age (ca. 130–110 Myr ago)" (*Spinostropheus gautieri*, MNN TIG6, an articulated trunk); early noasaurid and abelisaurid members at about 110 Myr; and "a hornless abelisaurid skull of early Late Cretaceous age (ca. 95 Myr ago)" (*Rugops primus*, MNN IGU1; Echkar Formation, Niger).
- *Rugops*: skull with textured surface and a row of seven small fenestrae (pits) along the snout roof; text calls it a "hornless" early abelisaurid.
- Phylogeny: 169 characters, 205 steps, retention index 0.93.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1 (paleogeographic globe).* A black-and-gray globe map of the Early Cenomanian (about 97 Myr), with land in gray, numbered dots for abelisaurid localities (1–6), lettered land bridges (A–C), and a dashed arrow for the high-latitude route. A single image summarizes the debate over when Africa split off.
- *Fig. 2 (*Spinostropheus*).* A line drawing of the whole presacral column as a long arc of vertebrae with labelled bones (C3, C10, D1, D5, D13, S1–S3) and a skeleton silhouette in the margin; insets b–d show individual vertebrae with leader lines. Technical and spare; the silhouette is the entry point.
- *Fig. 3 (*Rugops* skull).* Panels in three views, line drawing with stippled bone: dorsal view with seven numbered pits (3–6 are the labelled ones), a long low lateral skull, and a ventral/palate view with tooth sockets. Hard to appreciate without the caption; the pitted dorsal view is the interesting one.
- *Fig. 4 (tree with biogeography).* Cladogram on a geologic column with bars for stratigraphic range, with skull silhouettes at the right for Carnotaurinae, Abelisauridae and Abelisauroidea; numbered nodes link to the diagnosis. Africa and America are labelled by letters at the tips.

**Assessment.** The comparison with *Chenanisaurus* (paper 2) lets you present two competing claims about when Africa isolated; one is based on a skull, the other on a jaw.

## 7. Velandia et al. 2025: "A new Late Cretaceous abelisaurid species from La Rioja Province, northwestern Argentina" (*Vitosaura colozacani*)

**Central claim.** CRILAR-Pv 506, from the Campanian Los Llanos Formation at the Tama locality, is a new genus and species, and the first named theropod from the Cretaceous of La Rioja. Its position is unstable: "either a non-brachyrostran abelisaurid, closely related to Indo-Malagasy species in some trees, or a non-carnotaurinin furileusaurian."

**Key facts**
- Elements: first dorsal centrum, second dorsal vertebra, partial sacrum, left ilium, pubis and ischium, "and other indeterminate elements."
- Associated with titanosaurian sauropod remains and eggs. Geology: a section about 150 m thick of sandstones and conglomerates with paleosols; the specimen is near the base of the Los Llanos Formation.
- Abelisaurid record: "more than 25 species" described to date, mostly Patagonian.
- Bone histology: thin sections of the pubis; lines of arrested growth (LAGs) and secondary osteons are visible.
- "The interrelationships among abelisaurids are poorly supported."

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (geology).* Three panels: a stratigraphic column with a black theropod silhouette and red arrow at the fossil level, with a legend of sandstone and conglomerate symbols; a landscape photograph of low badlands with scrub (the Tama locality); and a close-up of a sauropod limb bone in the ground with a red-handled brush for scale.
- *Fig. 5 (pelvis).* Panel 1: a white/yellow reconstruction of the left hemipelvis, with a dashed outline for missing parts and labelled il, pu, is, ac, of. Panels 2–13: gray-brown bone fragments photographed on black from many angles, each with a 10 cm bar. The specimen is incomplete and weathered, and the figure makes that obvious.
- *Fig. 6 (histology).* Panel 1: pelvis outline marking where the section was cut. Panel 2: a round brown-gray slice with a diagram below it (orange growth rings around a gray medullary cavity, small numbered boxes marking where photos were taken). Panels 3–11: nine microscope views in bright magenta-pink and blue (crossed-polarized light), some with yellow arrows marking growth lines. The most colourful figure in the folder; the colours are an artifact of polarizing filters.
- Not viewed: Figs. 7–9 (consensus trees).

**Assessment.** A weakly supported new taxon, which is the useful part for a lesson on how much a new name needs. The authors are open about it.

## 8. Burch & Carrano 2012: "An articulated pectoral girdle and forelimb of the abelisaurid theropod *Majungasaurus crenatissimus*"

**Central claim.** The articulated forelimb of *Majungasaurus* (FMNH PR 2836) shows that abelisaurids share an extremely reduced forelimb with a unique morphology, unlike the typical theropod hand, and the authors reinterpret the manual digits of *Aucasaurus* and *Carnotaurus*.

**Key facts**
- "The radius and ulna are approximately one-quarter the length of the humerus and both have expanded proximal and distal articular surfaces relative to their narrow diaphyses."
- The manus has four digits, each with a short metacarpal and one (I, IV) or two (II, III) phalanges. "No ossified carpals." Minimum phalangeal formula 1/2/1–2/?/1.
- The scapulocoracoid is large and broad, with a long blade with parallel margins; "a seemingly incongruous size disparity given the highly reduced condition of its forelimbs."
- Proportions are "stout, more similar to the condition in Carnotaurus than in Aucasaurus."
- A furcula is described for the first time in this animal (Fig. 4).

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (reconstruction).* A gray CT-based 3D model in lateral view. The scapular blade sweeps up and back at left, big and flat; the coracoid curves in front of it; a tiny forelimb (humerus, radius, ulna, hand) hangs from the shoulder at the lower right, like a charm on a bracelet. Scale bar 5 cm. The forelimb-to-girdle size mismatch is the whole claim, and this figure shows it.
- *Fig. 8 (left manus).* Four rows of paired photographs and line drawings: gray bones in dorsal and ventral view, drawn beside them in outline with cross-hatching for broken surfaces and gray for matrix; metacarpals labelled MC I–IV, phalanges I-1, II-1, II-2, III-1, III-2?, IV-1. The question mark on III-2 is an honest uncertainty.
- *Figs. 9–10.* Proximal view of the four articulated metacarpals (a row of four blocky bones, MC IV to MC I) and a comparison of the first phalanx of digit III in two specimens, one with pathological swelling.

**Assessment.** A strong visual hook for the "why is the arm so small" question. Keep it to Fig. 2.

## 9. Rogers et al. 2003: "Cannibalism in the Madagascan dinosaur *Majungatholus atopus*"

**Central claim.** Tooth marks on *Majungatholus* (now *Majungasaurus*) bones match the dentition of *Majungatholus* itself, so the animal fed on its own species.

**Key facts**
- "At least 12 postcranial elements of Majungatholus preserved at this site [MAD96-01] exhibit tooth marks" (caudal vertebra, chevrons, ribs, neural arches).
- Another bonebed (MAD96-21) holds a subadult *Majungatholus*; "at least nine Majungatholus elements" there carry marks.
- Total: "at least 21 tooth-marked elements derived from two Majungatholus individuals (FMNH PR2100 and UA 8678) preserved in two separate localities."
- Marks: sets of parallel gouges and denticle drag marks. Denticle density in marked bone sets is 2.1 per mm (range 1.9–2.5, ten crowns measured). Tooth-mark spacing 1.0–1.7 cm, consistent with the spacing of *Majungatholus* teeth.
- Alternatives tested: the contemporaneous crocodyliforms (*Trematochampsa*, *Mahajangasuchus*) have blunt, conical, irregular teeth that could not make the marks; *Masiakasaurus* is too small.
- Also: tooth-marked sauropod *Rapetosaurus* pubis (MAD93-18) with parallel furrows.
- Open question the authors state: "Whether Majungatholus killed both individuals and thus practised intraspecific predation, or opportunistically scavenged the carcasses, is unknown."
- They also call Coelophysis cannibalism claims "unsubstantiated."

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* A montage of gray-scale bone photographs: a tall caudal vertebra at left with arrows to grooves, a chevron and rib laid horizontally above it, and smaller bone pieces below, black arrows at every tooth-mark set. The marks are subtle ruts on a pitted surface; the arrows are necessary.
- *Fig. 2.* Three stacked panels: a close-up of a serrated tooth crown (labelled "95061-1" in ink), a rib with white arrows at denticle drags, and the right dentary of FMNH PR2100 in lateral view with a bracket spanning four teeth to show the spacing. This is the direct match of marks to jaw.
- *Fig. 3.* A *Rapetosaurus* pubis with fine parallel furrows along its lower margin.
- *Fig. 4.* Four occlusal-view line drawings of dentaries (*Majungatholus*, *Masiakasaurus*, *Mahajangasuchus*, *Trematochampsa*) with circles of tooth positions. Round, irregular crocodile teeth versus evenly spaced theropod teeth: the alternative is eliminated by shape.

**Assessment.** The best claim / alternative / test sequence in the folder: marks, candidate causers, elimination. Ties directly to the transcript-style "how sure" slide: marks are strong evidence for feeding, weaker for killing versus scavenging. Pair with Baiano 2024 (paleopathology) for bite marks versus disease.

## 10. Pol et al. 2024: "A new abelisaurid dinosaur from the end Cretaceous of Patagonia and evolutionary rates among the Ceratosauria" (*Koleken inakayali*)

**Central claim.** *Koleken inakayali* from the La Colonia Formation is a new brachyrostran abelisaurid related to *Aucasaurus*, *Niebla* and *Carnotaurus*, and rates of morphological evolution across Ceratosauria are high in elaphrosaurine noasaurids and around the base of Abelisauridae.

**Key facts**
- Holotype MPEF-PV 10826: several skull bones, "an almost complete dorsal series, complete sacrum, several caudal vertebrae, pelvic girdle and almost complete hind limbs." The only other abelisaurid known from the formation is *Carnotaurus*.
- Eight diagnostic autapomorphies (smooth paradental plates; a row of foramina on the nasal; etc.).
- Histology: tibia section shows a thin external fundamental system only incompletely formed and a thin inner circumferential layer, so the individual was a subadult; minimum age 6 years. Nasal fusion and neurocentral sutures also suggest immaturity.
- Phylogeny: parsimony plus Bayesian tip-dating (fossilized birth-death). The Bayesian analysis places the origin of Furileusauria before the Cenomanian–Turonian boundary event.
- Rates: Noasauridae show "an early phase of accelerated evolution of the axial and hind limb skeleton in the Jurassic"; abelisaurids show "sustained high rates of cranial evolution during the Early Cretaceous."

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2 (maxillae).* Right and left maxillae in lateral and medial view: pitted brown-gray bone with a row of tooth sockets, labelled with arrows (interdental plates, neurovascular foramina). Four panels, 5 cm bar.
- *Fig. 17 (histology).* (a) A round cross-section of the tibia, orange-brown, with a black medullary cavity and a white crack. (b) Primary bone with white arrowheads marking lines of arrested growth. (c) Green and yellow cross-polarized light showing parallel-fibred bone. (d) A thin inner circumferential layer by the cavity. The arrowheads in (b) are how you count years.
- *Fig. 21 (rates by body region).* Four tree panels (cranial, axial, forelimb, hind limb), each a phylogeny on a time scale (Late Triassic to Oligocene), with branches coloured from pale yellow through orange and magenta to black by mean evolutionary rate, and a column of coloured squares at right showing which taxa have data. The cranial panel has black and dark-purple branches at the base of Abelisauridae; the forelimb panel is mostly pale. Colour does the work, but the legend is small.
- Not viewed: the other 20 figures (bone-by-bone photographs, trees).

**Assessment.** Too long for a deck as a whole; two slides: a specimen plate, and Fig. 21 as a "which part of the body evolved fastest" prediction.

## 11. D'Emic et al. 2019: "Evolution of high tooth replacement rates in theropod dinosaurs"

**Central claim.** *Majungasaurus*, *Allosaurus* and *Ceratosaurus* all replaced teeth quickly, and the abelisaurid's rate is as fast as those of derived herbivores.

**Key facts**
- Replacement rates: *Majungasaurus* 56 days; *Allosaurus* 104 days; *Ceratosaurus* 107 days.
- Tooth formation times: 293 days (*Majungasaurus*), 359 (*Allosaurus*), 337 (*Ceratosaurus*).
- Largest dental histology and CT dataset for any theropod: over a dozen toothed elements of *Majungasaurus* sectioned or scanned.
- Incremental lines (von Ebner) are 18 ± 5 µm in *Majungasaurus*, 23 ± 1 µm in *Allosaurus*, 14 ± 3 µm in *Ceratosaurus*.
- Model: tooth age against apicobasal length is "nearly identical" to *Camarasaurus*, "substantially different" from *Diplodocus* (r² = 0.95 for *Majungasaurus*).
- No relationship between body mass and replacement rate (OLS r² = 0.024, p = 0.54; still not significant when only herbivores are used, r² = 0.53, p = 0.07).
- Interpretation (the authors' hypothesis): the high rate "may be a response to high rates of tooth wear in *Majungasaurus*."

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* Top row (a–c): three gray 3D CT surface renderings of jaw fragments with teeth (*Allosaurus*, *Ceratosaurus*, *Majungasaurus*). Bottom row (d–f): thin-section photographs in golden-amber; fine parallel daily growth lines run diagonally in each. 100 µm scale. The jump from skull to microscope is the best part.
- *Fig. 2.* Scatter of tooth formation time (days, y) against tooth length (mm, x), with three polynomial curves and each curve's equation printed beside a silhouette: *Diplodocus* (triangles, lowest), *Camarasaurus* (squares) and *Majungasaurus* (circles) almost overlapping. R² printed on each.
- *Fig. 3.* A: a phylogeny of dinosaurs on a time axis with branch colour showing log replacement rate (green slower through orange to red faster; blue for the theropods, *Tyrannosaurus* 777 and albertosaurine 454 days), and the rate in days written at each tip. B: scatter of ln body mass against ln replacement rate; the blue (carnivore) and yellow (herbivore) polygons overlap and silhouettes mark each point. The overlap is the finding.

**Assessment.** A negative result ("no relationship with body mass") plus a positive one (fast turnover). Useful as a test of an intuition students probably hold: big animals replace teeth slowly.

## 12. Mazzetta et al. 2009: "Cranial mechanics and functional interpretation of the horned carnivorous dinosaur *Carnotaurus sastrei*"

**Central claim.** Finite element analysis of the *Carnotaurus* skull indicates a comparatively weak but fast bite, a skull that could withstand a hatchet-like strike, and a skull that "could not have endured severe, rapid frontal blows during agonistic encounters."

**Key facts**
- FE model of the cranium built from 130 CT slices at 5 mm; 488,444 elements, 98,375 nodes; bone material assumed like bovine (Young's modulus 10 GPa, Poisson's ratio 0.4).
- Four load cases: three bite positions plus a frontal blow on the horns, applied bilaterally.
- Maximum adductor-muscle force 8,503 N (model 1). Maximum bite force at the posterior teeth: 3,341 N (model 2 conservative) up to 5,274 N, with condylar force 11,190 N (model with muscle pinnation and other features accounted for). Those numbers are 58% and 88% higher than model 1.
- Velocity ratios 0.056 and 0.081 for the posterior and anterior bite versus 0.160 and 0.225 for *Allosaurus*/*Caiman*: "faster than Allosaurus fragilis."
- The adductor musculature reduces stress on the braincase during biting.
- Limits stated: bone material properties of dinosaurs are poorly known; muscle reconstruction affects the results.

**Figures** (all in gray scale)
- *Fig. 1.* Two photographs of the skull, lateral (large) and anterior (smaller, at right): short, deep skull, the bony horns above the eyes standing out sideways; labels for AOF, ITF, etc.
- *Fig. 3.* A pencil sketch of the skull with thick white arrows for muscle forces (F₁, F₂, F₃), jaw joint (F_A) and prey reaction (F_B), with angle marks. This is the model's free-body diagram.
- *Fig. 4.* Two skull FE plots (A above, B below), light-to-dark gray shading for stress with a legend (MPa), white arrows for muscle loads, and black X marks for where the model is pinned; the front teeth are shown dark but the caption says tooth stress is an artifact. Nothing here is colourful; you would need to redraw.
- *Figs. 5 and 6.* The frontal-blow results: a lateral skull with high stress concentrated near the horn base, then a cross-section through the horn region with a downward arrow (F_H) and the highest-stress zone shaded light.

**Assessment.** The headbutt conclusion conflicts with Delcourt 2018 (paper 17), which suggests derived abelisaurids could have had "low-displacement headbutting matches." Presenting these two together is a ready-made "do these two claims agree?" slide. The conflict is not a contradiction in the data: Mazzetta tested severe rapid blows; Delcourt proposes low-displacement ones.

## 13. Gates, Organ & Zanno 2016: "Bony cranial ornamentation linked to rapid evolution of gigantic theropod dinosaurs"

**Central claim.** In theropods, lineages with bony cranial ornaments evolved large body size an order of magnitude faster than lineages without them, and ornaments do not originate below a body-mass threshold.

**Key facts**
- Phylogenetic comparative framework: 111 theropod species (the t-test sample); body mass 0.1 to 47,000 kg.
- Bayesian phylogenetic t-test: posterior slope range 0.5 to 3 (median 1.83); "strong support for the hypothesis that large body size evolves coincidently with" ornaments.
- Rate of body-size evolution toward giantism "an order of magnitude faster (×20 faster)" in ornamented lineages.
- Threshold: a minimum of 55.2 kg from the PGLS analysis (the lowest estimate, 36 kg, is the body mass of a sheep-like comparison).
- Maniraptoriformes "generally lack osseous cranial ornaments despite repeatedly crossing this body size threshold," consistent with a shift to display using pennaceous feathers.
- Authors define ornament versus weapon but say fossils cannot tell which a given structure was.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* A: strip plot of ln body mass (y) with blue closed circles for unornamented species, red for ornamented, open circles for non-maniraptoriform species, box plots at left, a horizontal line at about 4.2, and two skull silhouettes (a blue, plain skull and a red, crested one). B–D: three small black histograms for slope (peak at 1.82), punctuation (0.31) and phylogenetic signal (near 1.0, piled against the right edge). Red dots cluster at the top; the pattern is visible without statistics.
- *Fig. 2.* Left: a large tall phylogeny of non-avian theropods, branch colour blending blue (no ornament) to red (ornament), with clade names (Paraves, Oviraptorosauria, Tyrannosauroidea, etc.). Right: a body-mass bar for every tip, blue or red; the giant red bars belong to Tyrannosauroidea and Carnosauria. Green shading and a feather icon at the top mark where pennaceous feathers appear. Crowded but informative.

**Assessment.** The only paper that puts the abelisaur horns in a theropod-wide context. Caveats: correlation, not cause; the sample is not abelisaur-specific.

## 14. Baiano et al. 2023: "Osteology of the axial skeleton of *Aucasaurus garridoi*: phylogenetic and paleobiological inferences"

**Central claim.** The most complete caudal series known for a brachyrostran abelisaurid, with several new autapomorphies and, in CT scans, the first reported pneumatic traits in an abelisaurid.

**Key facts**
- Holotype MCF-PVPH-236, Anacleto Formation (lower Campanian), Patagonia.
- Autapomorphies: "pneumatic recesses on the dorsal surface of the anterior caudal neural arches, a tubercle lateral to the prezygapophysis of mid caudal vertebrae, a marked protuberance on the lateral rim of the transverse process of the caudal vertebrae, and ... a small ligamentous scar near the anterior edge of the dorsal surface in the anteriormost caudal transverse process."
- 17 new scorings for *Aucasaurus*; matrix of 246 characters and 46 taxa. The strict consensus is "quite unresolved" and the reduced consensus puts *Aucasaurus* in the brachyrostran furileusaurian clade.
- Other axial features: a sacrum with T-shaped neural spine tops; fused sacral neural spines.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* A black-and-white silhouette of *Aucasaurus* (long tail, small arms), with the preserved axial bones in white inside the dark body outline, 1 m scale bar. Immediately shows how much of the animal is known.
- *Fig. 27.* Two cladograms side by side, A (the unresolved strict consensus, a long comb with coloured dots at nodes: Neotheropoda red, Averostra green, etc.) and B (the reduced consensus, with taxon names in a more resolved form, coloured arrows for stem-based taxa).
- *Fig. 33.* A: a vertebra photograph with a black box and dashed lines leading to an enlarged inset; the inset shows a fine honeycomb of orange tissue (camellate bone). B: another vertebra, same treatment. This is the pneumaticity evidence.
- Not viewed: the other 31 figures, which are mostly vertebra-by-vertebra plates.

**Assessment.** Specialist anatomy; use Fig. 1 and Fig. 33 only.

## 15. Persons & Currie 2011: "Dinosaur speed demon: the caudal musculature of *Carnotaurus sastrei*"

**Central claim.** *Carnotaurus* had a larger caudofemoralis muscle for its body size than any other theropod yet studied, which suggests good short-burst sprinting, with a stiff tail that would have limited tight turns.

**Key facts**
- Anterior caudal ribs project at a high dorsolateral angle and interlock at their tips in *Carnotaurus*, *Aucasaurus* and (less so) *Skorpiovenator*; this "facilitated the expansion of the caudal hypaxial musculature at the expense of the epaxial musculature."
- Ridges on *Aucasaurus* caudal ribs are interpreted as attachment scars for the intra-caudofemoralis/ilio-ischiocaudalis septa.
- Conservative model, MACN-CH 894: caudofemoralis 222 kg; ilio-ischiocaudalis 63 kg; longissimus 15 kg; spinalis 7 kg; total tail muscle 307 kg. With a body mass of 1,500 kg, caudofemoralis is 14.8% of body mass and 72.3% of tail muscle. Table 2 comparisons: *Tyrannosaurus* (5,622 kg) 9.3% of body mass; *Gorgosaurus* (400 kg) 4.3%; *Ornithomimus* (150 kg) 6.6%.
- "A progressive sequence of increasing caudofemoral mass and tail rigidity among the Abelisauridae of South America."
- The authors say the speed claim is an inference from muscle size, not a measurement of speed.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* Line drawing of caudal vertebra 6 in three views: lateral (a big neural spine and a flaring caudal rib), anterior (a "V"/butterfly shape, the ribs sweeping up and out), and dorsal. Labelled with 5 cm scale.
- *Fig. 5.* A cross-section through the tail: a red-and-orange oval packed with concentric whorls of muscle; a small white vertebra and chevron in the middle; the big oval below the centre labelled M. caudofemoralis, the thin upper wedges M. spinalis and M. longissimus, M. ilio-ischiocaudalis at the sides. The size of the lower oval does the argument.
- *Table 2.* Not a figure, but the key numbers (see above).
- *Fig. 7.* A painted life restoration (by Lida Xing and Yi Liu): a red-brown, striped *Carnotaurus* mid-stride with mouth open, tail raised and a bulky, muscular rear.

**Assessment.** This is a strong visual-argument paper. Check against Mazzetta (weak bite, quick snap) for a consistent "fast, light-headed hunter" story, but note both are model-based.

## 16. Baiano et al. 2024: "New information on paleopathologies in non-avian theropod dinosaurs: a case study on South American abelisaurids"

**Central claim.** Three Patagonian abelisaurids show distinct pathologies, and across non-avian theropods the distribution of injuries is strongly associated with taxon and body region, suggesting different lifestyles and behaviours.

**Key facts**
- *Aucasaurus garridoi*: two fused caudal vertebrae from a failure of vertebral segmentation (a congenital malformation), "the first case ... documented so far in non-tetanuran theropods."
- *Elemgasem nubilus* (MCF-PVPH-380): fused caudal vertebrae with an intervertebral space visible in histology, interpreted as spondyloarthropathy, also a first for a non-tetanuran.
- *Quilmesaurus curriei* (MPCA-PV-100): right tibia with a band of radial fibrolamellar bone; cause uncertain, "it could be a response to some kind of pathology."
- Database: "more than 337 bone[s] affected by pathology" in 39 genera and 18 families. Tyrannosauridae about 37%, Allosauridae about 23%, Carcharodontosauridae 9.8%, Abelisauridae about 8%. Traumatic injuries are most common, mainly in axial elements of large theropods.
- A positive correlation between osteomyelitis and Abelisauridae (mainly in the skull and axial skeleton), possibly a physiological tendency.
- Methods: histology, CT scans (120 kVp, 50 mA, 5 mm slices), macroscopic examination.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* An *Aucasaurus* skeleton silhouette in black and white with coloured highlights (green leg, orange and blue tail segments), red dotted lines from each to a boxed photograph of the pathological bone: A (tibia, a long cream bone with a swelling), B (a vertebra with haemal arch), C and D (fused tail vertebrae). The leader lines make the location of every lesion obvious.
- *Fig. 8.* A pie chart of pathologies by family: a huge blue slice (Tyrannosauridae), orange (Allosauridae), gray (Carcharodontosauridae), yellow (Abelisauridae), and many thin slices labelled with rotated text. The labels are crowded; the sample bias (more specimens of tyrannosaurids) is not controlled in the picture.
- *Fig. 11.* A painted illustration of three abelisaurids on black: a large one at upper left and a smaller one coiled below, with the pathological body parts picked out in a gold cuff or bracket. Decorative but memorable.
- Not viewed: Figs. 2–7 (CT, histology) and 9–10 (graphs).

**Assessment.** Good for "what can a broken bone tell us." The authors note taphonomic doubts about some marks. The sampling bias in Fig. 8 deserves a slide.

## 17. Delcourt 2018: "Ceratosaur palaeobiology: new insights on evolution and ecology of the southern rulers"

**Central claim.** A review-and-inference paper: ceratosaurs ruled the Southern Hemisphere, abelisaurids lost grasping ability, and soft tissue on the head (cornified sheaths, armour-like dermis, scales) may have supported "low-displacement headbutting matches" in derived taxa like *Majungasaurus* and *Carnotaurus*.

**Key facts**
- Two main branches: Noasauridae and a new name, Etrigansauria (Ceratosauridae + Abelisauridae).
- Abelisaurids "have lost the ability to grasp in the most derived taxa, but the reduced forelimb might have had some display function."
- *Limusaurus*: "lost all their teeth and probably changed the dietary preference at maturity."
- Rugosities on skull bones are interpreted as marks of overlying soft tissues: scales, armour-like dermis, cornified cover and sheaths; inferred from comparison with crocodiles, birds and *Tyrannosaurus*.
- Inferences are indirect and the paper says some taxa are doubtful (e.g., *Kryptops* may be a *nomen dubium*).

**Figures**
- *Fig. 2.* Six photographs of ceratosaur skulls in lateral view (A *Ceratosaurus*, B *Skorpiovenator*, C *Carnotaurus*, D *Noasaurus*, E *Masiakasaurus*, F *Limusaurus*), on a gray or white background, with black scale bars; the first three are short and deep or horned, the last three small and slender. Lets you see the range of skull shapes in one frame.
- *Fig. 4.* Top row: three skull-roof photographs: A (*Rugops*, a long pitted nasal strip), B (*Abelisaurus*, a bulbous, rough nasal), C (*Carnotaurus*, a skull top with two horns sticking out as a "T"). Below each, a small skull diagram with green for scales, blue for armour-like dermis, red for cornified cover and yellow for cornified sheath. A neat image-plus-key design.
- *Fig. 6.* A watercolour-style painting: *Carnotaurus* above (yellow-orange scales with red marks, horns, small eyes) and *Pycnonemosaurus* below (greener, a bulky head), showing the skin the author infers. The painting is attractive and fully speculative.

**Assessment.** The soft-tissue claims are inference stacked on inference: the right place to show "how sure are we?". Compare to Hendrickx & Bell, where skin was actually preserved.

## 18. Langer et al. 2019: "A new desert-dwelling dinosaur (Theropoda, Noasaurinae) from the Cretaceous of south Brazil" (*Vespersaurus paranaensis*)

**Central claim.** A new noasaurine from the Caiuá Group (a mainly aeolian desert deposit) was just over 1 m long and "could have been functionally monodactyl, with a main central weight-bearing digit," which has not been recorded in archosaurs before but matches footprints from the same unit.

**Key facts**
- First dinosaur from the Caiuá Group; the best-preserved theropod from the Bauru Basin. Locality: Cruzeiro do Oeste, Paraná.
- Metatarsals II and IV are "very lateromedially compressed, as are the blade-like ungual phalanges of the respective digits."
- Phylogeny puts it in Noasaurinae; if *Noasaurus leali* is excluded it is the sister taxon of *Velocisaurus unicus*.
- The Caiuá Group had a scarce fossil record: only lizards, turtles and pterosaurs before this.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* Three-panel location map: South America with Paraná highlighted in black; a map of the state with the town marked; a hatched urban area with a hammer icon at the type locality. A rivers-and-roads sketch.
- *Fig. 5.* Panels a–m of vertebrae: tan, 3D-rendered neck vertebrae (a–e), orange-pink photographs of trunk neural arches (f–h) and plain-edged centra (i–m), with dozens of anatomical labels and 10 mm or 5 mm bars.
- *Fig. 10 (the holotype foot).* a: distal end of metatarsals II–IV in dorsal view, pale blue-green bone; b and c: the distal phalanges of digits III and IV in side view, long, curved, blade-like claws; d: the whole right foot as preserved, white-cream bones splayed against dark rock, with the central toe long and the outer toes slim. The claw shapes on the lateral toes are what the "monodactyl" argument depends on.
- Not viewed: Figs. 2–4, 6–9, 11–13.

**Assessment.** Pairs with footprints (an independent line of evidence), which is the best test in the paper.

## 19. Canale et al. 2009: "New carnivorous dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous of NW Patagonia and the evolution of abelisaurid theropods" (*Skorpiovenator bustingorryi*)

**Central claim.** A nearly complete skeleton from the Huincul Formation (late Cenomanian–early Turonian) nests deeply inside a new clade of South American abelisaurids, Brachyrostra; and teeth previously referred to post-Cenomanian carcharodontosaurids "most probably belong to abelisaurids."

**Key facts**
- Holotype MMCH-PV 48, preserved length 4.35 m (premaxilla to 12th caudal), total estimate about 6 m.
- 19 maxillary teeth (more than other abelisaurids). Arcuate enamel wrinkles next to the serrations, similar to carcharodontosaurids.
- Eight autapomorphies listed (maxilla/jugal contact subvertical, lacrimal projected rostrally, etc.).
- Phylogeny: 17 taxa, 113 characters, TNT; single tree L = 171, CI = 0.69, RI = 0.826.
- Brachyrostran trend: skull shortening and skull-roof hyperossification correlated with orbit enclosure, "possibly related to shock-absorbing capabilities"; horns and cranial thickening "appear to be convergently acquired."
- Name from "skorpios" (scorpion) because scorpions were abundant at the dig.

**Figures**
- *Fig. 1.* a: a three-part map (South America, Argentina and the Neuquén area, with the dig site starred near El Chocón); b: a photograph of the skeleton still in its plaster jacket, a curled line of vertebrae and ribs on dark rock, 40 cm bar; c: a black silhouette with white bones of the whole animal, a 1 m bar. The jacket photo is the best "this is what a fossil looks like when you find it" image here.
- *Fig. 2.* a: skull photograph (gray, rugose, short and deep); b: a line drawing of the same skull with bone names; c–e: teeth (one is an isolated tooth long attributed to a carcharodontosaurid, shown for comparison).
- *Fig. 3.* A cladogram at left; at right a small *Skorpiovenator* skeleton surrounded by four photographs (a caudal vertebra, a skull piece, a proximal tibia, a distal tibia) with leader lines to numbered characters (79(1), 46(1), 100(1), 107(1)).

**Assessment.** The tooth-reassignment claim is an easy "what else could it be?" activity.

## Cross-paper threads for a deck

**What kind of animal.** Grillo (size), Burch & Carrano (arm), Hendrickx & Bell (skin), Persons & Currie (tail), Mazzetta (skull), Gates (horns).

**What did they eat, and how.** Rogers (marks), D'Emic (tooth turnover), Mazzetta (bite), Wang (*Limusaurus* diet shift).

**Where and when.** Sereno 2004, Longrich, Tortosa, Velandia, Langer, Canale, Pol (new taxa; each adds a point on a map).

**Life history.** Wang (ontogeny), Pol (histology of *Koleken*), Baiano 2024 (pathology).

**Tensions worth putting on one slide**
- Mazzetta: skull could not take severe head-butts. Delcourt: "low-displacement headbutting" with soft-tissue padding. Canale: horns and thickening "appear to be convergently acquired" and "possibly related to shock-absorbing capabilities." Three claims, three levels of evidence.
- Persons & Currie: sprinter. Mazzetta: weak, fast bite. Consistent, but both are model outputs.
- Sereno 2004 (Africa isolated after about 100 Myr ago) versus Longrich (endemic African lineage in the Maastrichtian) versus Tortosa (Europe and Africa as dispersal routes, crossing marine barriers).
- Hendrickx & Bell: skin preserved as scales. Delcourt: head tissue inferred from bone texture. Different grades of evidence about the same group.

**Numbers I would put on slides as given**
- 8.9 m (*Pycnonemosaurus*), 7.8 m (*Carnotaurus*).
- 20–65 mm feature scales; basement scales under 14 mm.
- 56 days (*Majungasaurus* tooth replacement) versus about 100 days (*Allosaurus*, *Ceratosaurus*).
- 222 kg caudofemoralis, 14.8% of body mass.
- At least 21 tooth-marked bones, two individuals, two quarries.
- 78 ontogenetic features; at least 42 teeth in stage I, toothless by stage IV.
- 55.2 kg threshold, ×20 faster.
- Forelimb: radius and ulna about one quarter of the humerus.

## Gaps and cautions
- I viewed 3–5 figures per paper, not all. Two long papers (Baiano 2023 and Pol 2024) are mostly bone-by-bone plates.
- Figure colours and layouts above describe the PDF pages as rendered; the journals' online versions may differ.
- Several summary numbers (BL estimates, rates, forces) are model outputs with their own uncertainty; I have kept the authors' own error terms where given.
- In paper 15, the percentages for the other theropods come from Table 2 as printed (*Ornithomimus* 6.6%, *Gorgosaurus* 4.3%, *Tyrannosaurus* 9.3% of body mass); I did not check them against the earlier paper they cite.
